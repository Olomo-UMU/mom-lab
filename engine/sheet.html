1. Aims

  1. Identify the parts of an inline four-cylinder engine and explain what each does.
  2. Find the crank layout and firing order by turning the engine by hand.
  3. Measure the bore, stroke, connecting-rod length and cylinder spacing, and use them to predict how far the piston moves for each crank angle. Check the prediction against measurements.
  4. Use the measured dimensions to work out piston velocity and acceleration, the turning moment diagram, the flywheel needed, and the unbalanced forces and couples of the engine.

2. Background

Each cylinder is a slider-crank: crank radius r = S/2, connecting rod length l, and n = l/r. With θ measured from top dead centre (TDC):

Piston distance from TDC: s = r + l − (r cosθ + √(l² − r² sin²θ)) ≈ r[(1 − cosθ) + (1 − cos2θ)/(4n)]
Piston velocity: v ≈ rω(sinθ + sin2θ/(2n)) · Piston acceleration: a ≈ rω²(cosθ + cos2θ/n)
Inertia force of the reciprocating mass m: F = m a = m rω² cosθ (primary) + (m rω²/n) cos2θ (secondary)
Crankshaft torque from a piston force F: T = F r sin(θ + φ)/cosφ, with sinφ = (r/l) sinθ
Flywheel: ΔE = I ω² Cs, where ΔE is the largest area between the torque curve and the mean torque line

In a four-stroke engine each cylinder fires once every 720° of crank rotation. In an inline four the cylinders fire 180° apart. The crank throws are usually at 0°, 180°, 180° and 0°, so pistons 1 and 4 move together, as do 2 and 3.

Safety

  • The cut edges of a sectioned engine are sharp. Do not run your fingers along them.
  • Keep fingers, pens and rules out of the gaps around the crankshaft, connecting rods, timing belt and valve gear while anyone is turning the engine.
  • Turn the engine only by hand, slowly, with a spanner or socket on the crankshaft pulley bolt, in the normal direction of rotation. Never force it. Say "turning" before you turn.
  • Tie back long hair and loose clothing. Report any loose parts to the technician.

3. Equipment

Cut-out inline four-cylinder engine; 300 mm steel rule; vernier caliper (with depth rod); inside caliper or telescopic bore gauge if available; spanner or socket and ratchet for the crankshaft pulley bolt; the paper degree wheel (last page), stuck to card; a pointer made from stiff wire fixed to the block with tape; marker pen; a device with the Four-Cylinder Engine Lab page open.

Part B. Crank layout and firing order

  1. Number the cylinders 1 to 4 starting from the timing-belt (pulley) end. Note which way the crankshaft turns when seen from the pulley end.
  2. Turn the crank slowly. Note which pistons reach the top together. Sketch the crankshaft seen from the end, showing the four throws.
  3. Bring piston 1 to the top. Look at its valves: if both are closed it is at firing TDC; if one is just closing and the other just opening it is at the exhaust–inlet overlap, so turn one more revolution.
  4. Keep turning. Each half turn, note which cylinder now has its piston at the top with both valves closed. That sequence is the firing order.
  5. Put a chalk mark on the camshaft pulley and on the crankshaft pulley. Count how many crank turns it takes for the camshaft to make one turn.

Part C. Measure the engine

  1. Bore B: measure the cylinder diameter at the top, middle and bottom of the bore, in two directions at right angles. (On a cut cylinder you can also measure the piston crown diameter and add about 0.05 mm.)
  2. Stroke S: bring the piston to the top. Measure the depth from the top face of the block (the deck) to the piston crown with the depth rod. Turn half a revolution and measure again at the bottom. S = depth at BDC − depth at TDC.
  3. Connecting rod length l: through the cut-out, measure from the centre of the small end (gudgeon pin) to the centre of the big end (crankpin), using the edges of the eyes: l = (outside to outside + inside to inside)/2.
  4. Cylinder spacing a: measure between the centres of neighbouring bores at the deck.
  5. Take three readings of each and work out the mean.

Part D. Piston displacement against crank angle

  1. Cut out the degree wheel, stick it to card, cut the centre and fix it to the crankshaft pulley with tape or under the pulley bolt washer. Fix the wire pointer to the block so its tip sits over the wheel's numbers.
  2. Find TDC accurately. Turn piston 1 up until it is about 5 mm below its top; note the wheel reading θa and the depth. Carry on over the top until the piston is back at exactly the same depth; note θb. TDC is halfway: θTDC = (θa + θb)/2. Turn the wheel on the pulley (not the crank) so the pointer reads 0 at TDC.
  3. Turn the crank in its running direction and measure the depth of piston 1 below the deck at every 30°, from 0° to 360°. The piston distance from TDC is s = depth − depth at TDC.
  4. Type the dimensions from Part C and these readings into the web page (the "Your measurements" table). They are plotted on the displacement graph.

5. Calculations

  1. Using your mean r and l, calculate s at every 30° by the exact and the approximate formula. Fill in the last rows of the Part D table. Plot measured, exact and approximate s against θ on one graph.
  2. Give two reasons why your measured values differ from the exact ones.
  3. At 3000 rpm, calculate by hand the piston velocity and acceleration at θ = 0°, 90° and 180°. Check your answers against the "At this crank angle" table on the web page.
  4. Take mrec = 0.54 kg. Calculate the amplitude of the primary and secondary inertia force for one cylinder at 3000 rpm. Draw the primary and secondary crank diagrams for your engine and show which forces and couples cancel. Find the resultant secondary force for the engine and compare it with the web page.
  5. From the turning moment diagram on the web page, explain how ΔE is found. Calculate the flywheel moment of inertia for Cs = 2% at 3000 rpm and compare with the web page.

6. Discussion

  1. Why does the piston spend more time near BDC than near TDC? Use your displacement graph.
  2. Why are the primary forces of a 0°-180°-180°-0° crankshaft balanced, but not the secondary forces? At what frequency does the engine shake, and how do some engines cancel it?
  3. What happens to the unbalanced force when the speed doubles? Why do engine designers care most about it at high speed?
  4. Why does a four-cylinder engine need a much smaller flywheel than a single-cylinder engine of the same power?
  5. At 6500 rpm the torque diagram looks very different from the one at 3000 rpm. Explain why, using the gas and inertia forces.
  6. Why is firing order 1-3-4-2 used rather than 1-2-3-4 on this crankshaft?

7. Report

Hand in: this sheet with all tables filled in; the displacement graph; the working for the calculations; the crank diagrams; answers to the discussion questions; and a short conclusion (about 150 words) on how well theory matched your measurements and what you learned about engine balance and flywheels.
