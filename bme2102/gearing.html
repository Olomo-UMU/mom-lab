5.1Why gears

Belts, ropes and friction wheels pass motion from one shaft to another by friction, so they can slip. When the speed ratio must stay exact, or the shafts are close together, we cut teeth on the wheels. A gear is a friction wheel whose teeth stop it slipping: the two pitch circles roll on each other exactly as the plain wheels would.

Friction wheels against gears

raise the load until the friction wheels slip

Advantages: exact velocity ratio; can carry large power; high efficiency; compact and reliable. Disadvantages: need special tools and machines to make; errors in the teeth cause vibration and noise; need lubrication; not suited to shafts far apart.

Materials: cast iron (good wear, cheap, for slow gears), steel (plain carbon or alloy, often heat-treated for strong teeth), phosphor bronze (worm wheels, to reduce wear of the worm), and non-metals such as nylon or laminated fabric for quiet running.

By the shafts

  • Parallel shafts: spur gears (teeth parallel to the axis), helical gears (teeth inclined at the helix angle: smoother and quieter but they push the shaft sideways) and herringbone or double-helical gears (two opposite helices, so the side thrusts cancel). Rack and pinion and internal gears also work between parallel lines.
  • Intersecting shafts: bevel gears, with teeth cut on cones. Equal bevel gears on shafts at right angles are mitre gears.
  • Shafts that neither meet nor are parallel: spiral (skew) gears and worm gears. A worm gives a large reduction in one step.

By peripheral speed and type of gearing

Low velocity below 3 m/s, medium 3 to 15 m/s, high above 15 m/s. In external gearing the wheels turn in opposite directions; in internal gearing they turn the same way; a rack is a gear of infinite radius that turns rotation into straight-line motion.

5.4Law of gearing

For the speed ratio to stay constant, the common normal at the point where two teeth touch must always pass through the same point on the line of centres, the pitch point P. Then ω1/ω2 = O2P/O1P = D2/D1 = T2/T1. Teeth that obey this are conjugate. Involute teeth obey it automatically: the normal to an involute is always tangent to its base circle, so every contact lies on the one common tangent MN of the two base circles.

Move the centres apart: the base circles do not change, so the ratio does not change, but the working pitch circles grow, the pressure angle grows, and backlash opens up. That is the first advantage of involute gears listed in 5.5.3.

O1M = O1P cos φ,   O2N = O2P cos φ,   O1O2 = (O1M + O2N)/cos φ
Tooth force F along MN: tangential FT = F cos φ, radial FR = F sin φ, torque = FT × r

5.5Involute and cycloidal teeth

An involute is traced by the end of a taut string unwound from a circle (the base circle). A cycloid is traced by a point on a circle rolling along a line; rolling on the outside of the pitch circle gives an epicycloid (the face of a cycloidal tooth), rolling on the inside gives a hypocycloid (the flank).

Drawing an involute

Involute against cycloidal

  • Involute: the centre distance can change a little without changing the ratio; the pressure angle stays constant during engagement (smooth running); each flank is a single curve and the basic rack has straight sides, so teeth are easy to cut. Its weakness is interference on pinions with few teeth.
  • Cycloidal: wider flanks, so stronger teeth for the same pitch (preferred for cast teeth); a convex face works on a concave flank, giving slightly less wear; no interference. But the centre distance must be exact and the pressure angle varies from a maximum to zero and back.

A larger pressure angle gives a tooth that is wider at the root, so it is stronger as a beam; a stub tooth is shorter, so it bends less and also avoids interference with fewer teeth. The 14.5° composite system has cycloidal tips and roots with an involute middle; it is strong but not interchangeable.

5.7Contact ratio

A pair of teeth touches from K, where the wheel’s addendum circle cuts MN, to L, where the pinion’s addendum circle cuts it. KP is the path of approach, PL the path of recess. The arc of contact is the path of contact ÷ cos φ, and the contact ratio = arc of contact ÷ circular pitch is the average number of pairs in contact. Above 1 the next pair always takes over before the last one lets go.

KP = √(RA² − R² cos² φ) − R sin φ    PL = √(rA² − r² cos² φ) − r sin φ
arc of contact = (KP + PL)/cos φ    contact ratio = arc of contact / (πm)
sliding velocity at a distance x from P = (ω1 + ω2) x   (zero at P)

5.8Interference

The involute exists only outside the base circle. M and N, where MN touches the base circles, are the interference points. If the wheel’s addendum circle cuts MN beyond M, the wheel tip would touch the pinion inside its base circle, where there is no involute: the tip digs into the root. When the pinion is cut by a rack or hob the cutter does exactly this and removes metal from the root, undercutting the tooth and weakening it. Interference is avoided when both addendum circles cut MN between M and N.

Cutting a pinion with a rack cutter

Largest path of approach MP = r sin φ, of recess PN = R sin φ, so the largest path of contact MN = (r + R) sin φ
Largest arc of contact = (r + R) tan φ
