2.1Links and constrained motion

A machine is not a structure: (1) its parts move relative to one another, (2) it turns the energy it receives into useful work, and (3) its links carry motion and power, whereas the members of a structure carry forces only. We study a machine by fixing one part and finding how the others move relative to it.

Each part that moves relative to another is a kinematic link (or element). A link must have relative motion and must be a resistant body, able to carry the forces without noticeable deformation. Links are rigid (connecting rod, crankshaft), flexible (belts, ropes, chains: tension only) or fluid (hydraulic presses, jacks and brakes: pressure only).

Types of constrained motion

Completely constrained: the motion is in one definite direction whatever the direction of the force, such as a square bar in a square hole, or a shaft with collars in a round hole. Incompletely constrained: more than one motion is possible, such as a plain round shaft in a round hole, which can turn or slide. Successfully constrained: the pair alone allows more than one motion, but something else completes the constraint, such as a load that stops a shaft lifting out of a foot-step bearing, the spring on an engine valve, or a piston in its cylinder.

2.1.2Kinematic pairs

Two links in contact form a kinematic pair. Pairs are classified three ways.

  • By relative motion: sliding (piston and cylinder, tailstock on a lathe bed), turning (shaft in a bearing, cycle wheel on its axle), rolling (ball and roller bearings), screw (lead screw and nut, bolt and nut), spherical (ball and socket, car mirror mount).
  • By contact: a lower pair has surface contact (sliding, turning and screw pairs); a higher pair has line or point contact, partly rolling and partly sliding (gear teeth, belts, ball bearings, cam and follower).
  • By closure: a self-closed pair is held together mechanically (all lower pairs); a force-closed pair is kept in contact by an outside force such as a spring or gravity (cam and follower).

2.1.3Kinematic chains

A kinematic chain is a set of pairs joined so that each link is part of two pairs and the relative motion is completely or successfully constrained. The crankshaft in its bearings, the crank and connecting rod, the rod and piston, and the piston and cylinder form four pairs; together they make a chain. For lower pairs, with l links, p pairs and j joints:

l = 2p − 4     j = (3/2) l − 2
With h higher pairs:   j + h/2 = (3/2) l − 2
Left side greater than the right: a locked chain (a structure). Equal: a kinematic chain. Less: an unconstrained chain.

Binary, ternary and quaternary joints

A binary joint joins two links. A ternary joint joins three and counts as two binary joints, since one link carries the pin for the other two. A quaternary joint joins four and counts as three. In general a joint of n links counts as n − 1 binary joints. Click the joints of the chain in Figure 2.4.

2.2Mechanisms and degrees of freedom

Fixing one link of a kinematic chain makes a mechanism. With four links it is a simple mechanism; with more, a compound mechanism. When it transmits power or does work it becomes a machine. Fixing each link in turn gives as many mechanisms as there are links: these are inversions.

The degrees of freedom (movability) are the number of inputs that must be controlled independently. A free link in a plane has three: x, y and θ. Pinning it to the fixed link leaves only θ, so a turning pair destroys two. Add the joints of a four-bar one at a time and watch the freedom fall from 9 to 1 (Figure 2.6).

Joining the links of a four-bar

Kutzbach criterion

With l links, one fixed, the l − 1 moving links start with 3(l − 1) freedoms. Each lower pair (binary joint) j removes 2 and each higher pair h removes 1:

n = 3(l − 1) − 2j − h    and with no higher pairs   n = 3(l − 1) − 2j
Grubler's criterion (n = 1, h = 0):   3l − 2j − 4 = 0
A mechanism of movability 1 with only single-freedom joints cannot have an odd number of links. The simplest are the four-bar and the slider-crank: l = 4, j = 4.

2.2.3The four-bar chain and Grashof's law

The most important chains have four lower pairs, each sliding or turning: the four-bar chain, the single slider-crank chain and the double slider-crank chain. The four-bar (quadric cycle) chain has four turning pairs. Grashof's law: for continuous relative rotation, the sum of the shortest and longest links must not exceed the sum of the other two, s + l ≤ p + q. The shortest link can then turn fully relative to the others; as the input it is the crank (driver). A link that only rocks is the lever, rocker or follower; the link joining crank and lever is the connecting rod or coupler; the fixed link is the frame.

Fixing different links gives the inversions in Figure 2.8: the beam engine (crank and lever), the locomotive coupling rod (double crank) and Watt's indicator (double lever).

2.2.3The single slider-crank chain

Replace one turning pair of the four-bar with a sliding pair and you have the single slider-crank chain: one sliding pair and three turning pairs, as in the reciprocating engine (Figure 2.9). Link 1 is the frame, 2 the crank, 3 the connecting rod and 4 the cross-head; the crank turns and the cross-head slides in its guides. It turns rotation into reciprocation and back.

Fixing each link in turn gives its inversions: the pendulum pump or bull engine (the cross-head, which carries the cylinder in that drawing, fixed), the oscillating cylinder engine and the crank and slotted lever quick return motion (the connecting rod fixed), and the rotary (Gnome) engine and the Whitworth quick return motion (the crank fixed). 2.2.3Quick return mechanisms

In a shaping or slotting machine the tool should cut slowly and come back quickly. In the crank and slotted lever mechanism the crank CB turns at a steady speed about C; a block on the crank pin slides in the slotted lever AP, which rocks about A, and a short link PR drives the ram along the line of stroke R1R2, perpendicular to AC produced. At the ends of the stroke the lever is tangent to the crank circle. The cut takes the crank through β, the return through α:

Time of cutting stroke / time of return stroke = β/α = (360° − α)/α,   with cos(α/2) = CB/AC
Length of stroke R1R2 = 2 AP sin(90° − α/2) = 2 AP × CB/AC

Crank and slotted lever quick return

In the Whitworth mechanism the crank CD is the fixed link. The driving crank CA turns about C; its block slides in the slotted lever, which turns completely about D. A rod PR drives the ram along a line through D perpendicular to CD. The cut happens while the crank turns through α and the return through β, so the time ratio is α/β = (360° − β)/β, and the stroke is 2 PD.

Whitworth quick return

2.2.3The double slider-crank chain

Two turning pairs and two sliding pairs make the double slider-crank chain. Its inversions are the elliptical trammels, the Scotch yoke and Oldham's coupling.

Elliptical trammels

A plate with two grooves at right angles is fixed. Two sliders run in the grooves and are pinned to a bar AB. With the bar at θ to the horizontal, a point P on it is at x = AP cos θ, y = BP sin θ, so x²/AP² + y²/BP² = 1: P draws an ellipse with semi-axes AP and BP. If P is the midpoint, AP = BP and it draws a circle.

Elliptical trammels

Scotch yoke

Fixing link 1 (or 3) gives the Scotch yoke, which turns rotation into reciprocation. The crank turns about B, its pin slides in the slotted yoke, and the yoke moves back and forth in its guides. Its displacement is exactly r cos θ: simple harmonic motion.

Scotch yoke

Oldham's coupling

Oldham's coupling joins two parallel shafts a small distance apart so that both turn at the same speed. Each shaft carries a flange with a slot; a middle disc has a tongue on each face, at right angles, that slides in the slots. The flanges and disc all turn at the same angular velocity, while each tongue slides in its slot. With ω the shaft speed and r the distance between the axes, the greatest sliding speed of each tongue is v = r ω.
