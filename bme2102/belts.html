4.1Belts and belt drives

Belts and ropes carry power from one shaft to another by pulleys turning at the same or different speeds. The power a belt can carry depends on (1) the belt speed, (2) the tension it is fitted at, (3) the arc of contact on the smaller pulley, and (4) the conditions it works in.

  • The shafts must be in line, so the tension is even across the belt.
  • The pulleys should not be too close, so the arc of contact on the smaller pulley stays large, nor too far apart, or the belt's weight loads the bearings and a long belt swings and runs off. For flat belts keep the centre distance at most 10 m and at least 3.5 times the larger pulley diameter.
  • Put the tight side at the bottom: sag on the slack side then increases the arc of contact.

The choice of drive depends on the shaft speeds and the speed ratio, the power, the centre distance, whether the drive must be positive, the shaft layout, the space and the service conditions. Flat belts suit moderate power with pulleys up to about 8 m apart; V-belts moderate power with pulleys close together; circular belts or ropes large power over more than 8 m. Belts are made of leather, cotton or fabric, rubber or balata: strong, flexible, durable and with a high coefficient of friction.

4.2Velocity ratio, slip and creep

With no slip the same length of belt passes over each pulley in a minute, π d1 N1 = π d2 N2, so

Velocity ratio   N2/N1 = d1/d2    with belt thickness t:   N2/N1 = (d1 + t)/(d2 + t)
Compound drive   N4/N1 = (d1 × d3)/(d2 × d4): speed of the last driven / speed of the first driver = product of driver diameters / product of driven diameters

Slip. If the grip is not enough, the driver can move without carrying the belt, or the belt without carrying the follower. With s1% slip between driver and belt and s2% between belt and follower, and s = s1 + s2 (the small product s1s2 neglected):

N2/N1 = (d1/d2)(1 − s1/100)(1 − s2/100) ≈ (d1/d2)(1 − s/100)    or with thickness   ((d1 + t)/(d2 + t))(1 − s/100)
Because belts slip, they are not used where an exact ratio matters (the hands of a clock).

Creep. The belt stretches as it passes from the slack side to the tight side and contracts on the way back, so it creeps relative to the pulley surfaces. With σ1, σ2 the stresses on the tight and slack sides and E the Young's modulus of the belt:

N2/N1 = (d1/d2) × (E + √σ2)/(E + √σ1)

4.5Length of a belt and angle of contact

Open belt (both pulleys turn the same way). The belt leaves the larger pulley at E and G and the smaller at F and H. With r1, r2 the radii and x the centre distance, sin α = (r1 − r2)/x, and L = 2(arc JE + EF + arc FK). Using α ≈ sin α and the binomial expansion of EF = √(x² − (r1 − r2)²):

L = π(r1 + r2) + 2x + (r1 − r2)²/x    = (π/2)(d1 + d2) + 2x + (d1 − d2)²/4x

Crossed belt (the pulleys turn opposite ways). Now sin α = (r1 + r2)/x and

L = π(r1 + r2) + 2x + (r1 + r2)²/x    = (π/2)(d1 + d2) + 2x + (d1 + d2)²/4x
A crossed belt depends only on r1 + r2, so stepped pulleys for a crossed belt just need the sum of each pair of radii to be the same.

Angle of contact (4.8). For a crossed belt it is the same on both pulleys: θ = (180° + 2α) π/180 rad with sin α = (r1 + r2)/x. For an open belt it is (180° − 2α) on the smaller pulley and (180° + 2α) on the larger, with sin α = (r1 − r2)/x. The belt slips first on the pulley with the smaller arc, so that is the one used in the ratio of tensions.

4.6Power and the ratio of driving tensions

The driver pulls the belt from one side and delivers it to the other, so the tight side tension T1 is larger than the slack side tension T2. The effective driving force at the rim is T1 − T2, so

P = (T1 − T2) v   watts    torque on the driver (T1 − T2) r1, on the follower (T1 − T2) r2

Ratio of tensions (4.7). Take a short piece of belt PQ over an angle δθ, with tension T at one end and T + δT at the other, a normal reaction RN and friction μRN. Resolving radially, RN = T δθ; resolving along the belt, μRN = δT. So δT/T = μ δθ, and integrating from T2 to T1 over the angle of contact:

loge(T1/T2) = μθ    T1/T2 = eμθ    or   2.3 log10(T1/T2) = μθ
θ in radians. The tension grows exponentially round the arc, which is why a little more wrap or friction gives a lot more grip.

4.9Initial tension and maximum power

Initial tension. A belt is fitted with an initial tension T0 so it grips at rest. Running, one side tightens to T1 and the other slackens to T2. If the belt is perfectly elastic and its length stays the same, the stretch on one side equals the contraction on the other, T1 − T0 = T0 − T2:

T0 = (T1 + T2)/2   (neglecting centrifugal tension)    T0 = (T1 + T2 + 2TC)/2   (with it)

Maximum power (4.10). A running belt of mass m per metre carries a centrifugal tension TC = m v² on both sides, which does no useful work. If T is the largest tension the belt may carry, the tension left for driving is T1 = T − TC, and with C = 1 − 1/eμθ:

P = (T1 − T2) v = T1 C v = (T v − m v³) C    dP/dv = 0 ⇒ T − 3 m v² = 0
So for maximum power   TC = T/3,   T1 = 2T/3,   v = √(T/3m)
Run faster than this and centrifugal tension eats the driving tension; at v = √(T/m) the belt carries no power at all.

4.11V-belt drive

V-belts are fabric and cord moulded in rubber, trapezoidal in section and made endless, with an included angle usually 30° to 40°. They run in grooved pulleys and touch only the sides of the groove, with clearance at the bottom for wear. The wedging in the groove raises the grip and so the power, for the same friction, arc and tension. Several belts can run side by side; they must stretch equally, so a set is replaced together.

Advantages: compact (short centre distance); the drive is positive, with negligible slip; smooth, as there are no joints; long life, 3 to 5 years; easy to fit and remove; quiet; cushions shock at starting; velocity ratios up to 10; high limiting ratio of tensions; runs either way up, at any angle. Disadvantages: not for large centre distances; less durable than flat belts; grooved pulleys cost more to make; creep makes them unsuitable for constant-speed work such as synchronous machines and timing; life depends on temperature, tension and matched lengths; centrifugal tension limits them to about 5 to 50 m/s.

Ratio of tensions. With R1 the normal reaction on each side of a groove of angle 2β, and R the total reaction in the plane of the groove, resolving gives R = 2R1 sin β. The friction force is 2μR1 = μR cosec β, so the flat-belt result holds with μ replaced by μ cosec β:

2.3 log(T1/T2) = μ θ cosec β    T1/T2 = eμθ cosec β
