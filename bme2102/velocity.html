3.1Instantaneous centres

A link that moves from AB to A1B1 both translates and rotates. The same move can be made as a pure rotation about one point I, where the perpendicular bisectors of AA1 and BB1 meet. As the move shrinks to an instant, I becomes the instantaneous centre (centro, or virtual centre). Its path in space is the space centrode; its path relative to the body is the body centrode. The line through I perpendicular to the plane is the instantaneous axis, and its locus is the axode.

Any plane move is a rotation about one point

Properties and number

Two rigid links rotate relative to each other about their instantaneous centre, and at that point they have no velocity relative to each other: it is a point common to both, with the same velocity relative to any third link. A chain of n links has N = n(n − 1)/2 instantaneous centres. Fixed centres stay put, permanent centres move but stay at a joint (together these are the primary centres), and centres that are neither fixed nor permanent (secondary) change with the configuration.

Locating them. (1) At a pin joint the centre is the pin. (2) For pure rolling it is the contact point, and velocities are in proportion to the distance from it: vA/vB = I12A/I12B. (3) For sliding it lies on the common normal: at infinity for a straight guide, at the centre of curvature for a curved one. Kennedy's theorem finds the rest: the three instantaneous centres of any three bodies lie on one straight line.

3.1.2Velocity by the instantaneous centre method

Every point of a link moves as if the link turned about its instantaneous centre, so v = r ω with r measured from that centre, and the velocity is perpendicular to r. For a four-bar there are 4 × 3/2 = 6 centres: I12, I23, I34 and I14 are found by inspection; Kennedy's theorem and the circle diagram give I13 and I24. Step through the procedure.

3.2The relative velocity method

For two bodies A and B, the velocity of A relative to B is vAB = vA − vB (vector ba = oa − ob), and vBA = −vAB. On a rigid link, the velocity of any point relative to another point of the same link is perpendicular to the line joining them, with vBA = ω × AB. A point C on AB divides ab in the same ratio as it divides AB: the velocity image.

Drawing the diagram. Take a pole o. Draw oa equal and parallel to the known vA. Through a draw a line perpendicular to AB (the direction of vBA); through o draw the known direction of vB. They meet at b. Then ωAB = ab/AB, and its sense follows from the direction of ab.

3.2.2The slider-crank mechanism and rubbing velocity

For a crank OB of radius r turning at ω, ob = vB = ω r, perpendicular to OB. The rod AB is rigid, so ba is perpendicular to AB; the slider moves along the line of stroke, so oa is along AO. Their meeting point gives vA = oa, and ωAB = ab/AB.

Rubbing velocity at a pin joint is the pin radius times the relative angular velocity of the two links: (ω1 − ω2) r if they turn the same way, (ω1 + ω2) r if they turn opposite ways.

3.3Forces in a mechanism and mechanical advantage

A force FA at joint A (in the direction of vA) is passed to joint B as FB. Ignoring friction and changes of kinetic energy, the power in equals the power out:

FA vA = FB vB    or with torques   TA ωA = TB ωB    with efficiency η:   η = TB ωB / TA ωA
Mechanical advantage (load/effort):   M.A.(ideal) = FB/FA = vA/vB = ωA/ωB    M.A.(actual) = η ωA/ωB
If a force does not act along the velocity of its point, use its component along the velocity.

3.4Acceleration analysis

When B moves relative to A on a rigid link that turns at ω with angular acceleration α, the acceleration of B relative to A has two parts: a radial (centripetal) part arBA = ω² AB = vBA²/AB, along the link towards A, and a tangential part atBA = α AB, perpendicular to the link. Their vector sum b′a′ is the acceleration image of the link.

The two components of acceleration

Drawing the acceleration diagram

From a pole o′ draw o′a′ for the known aA. From a′ draw a′x parallel to AB for arBA (worked out from the velocity diagram), then through x a line perpendicular to AB for the unknown atBA. Through o′ draw the known direction of aB; the lines meet at b′. Then αAB = atBA/AB. A third point C of the link is found by drawing triangle a′b′c′ similar to ABC.
