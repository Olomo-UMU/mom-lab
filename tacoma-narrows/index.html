Tacoma Narrows Bridge · 7 November 1940 · 42 mph wind · 0.2 Hz twisting

Tacoma Narrows Flutter Lab

The bridge did not fail by resonance. In a steady wind its twisting motion drew energy from the air on every cycle, which acts like negative damping, so a small twist grew until the deck was rotating tens of degrees. Run the self-excited model and the popular "vortex resonance" explanation side by side and see which one can produce what was filmed.

What happened

  • 1 Jul 1940Opens with a 2,800 ft centre span and a span-to-width ratio of 1 : 72. Its shallow plate-girder deck soon earns the name "Galloping Gertie" for its vertical waves.
  • 7 Nov, a.m.Wind of 38 mph at 7:30, then a steady 42 mph. The deck runs in vertical waves several feet high.
  • ≈ 10:00A cable connection at midspan slips. The motion switches to twisting: the two halves rotate in opposite directions about a still centre line, about 12 cycles per minute.
  • 10:03–11:02The twist persists for about an hour. The roadway tilts as much as 45°, with edges rising and falling many feet.
  • 11:02Suspenders snap and a 600 ft section falls into Puget Sound; most of the remaining centre span follows within minutes.

Why it was not resonance

Resonance needs an outside force repeating at the structure's natural frequency. At 42 mph the vortices shed by the deck repeat at about 1 Hz, five times faster than the 0.2 Hz twist. They line up only near 8 mph, where the air forces are too weak to matter. The federal investigation also found no sharp link between wind speed and oscillation frequency.

What did happen was self-excitation. The twisting deck shapes the airflow, including vortices that form at the leading edge, so that the air pushes in step with the motion. Above a threshold wind (about 35 mph once the midspan link had slipped) the energy gained each cycle exceeds what the structure dissipates. The effective damping turns negative and the amplitude grows in a steady wind, with no tuning required.

Model, calibration and references

One twisting mode, θ = rotation at quarter span (the antisymmetric mode has its node at midspan, so θ(x) = θ·sin(2πx/L)).

θ̈ + 2ω [ζs + ζlink + ζaero(U) + β θ²] θ̇ + ω² θ = M(t)

Self-excited flutter: M = 0 and the air adds damping that falls with wind speed and turns negative.

ζaero(U) = −a U²,  flutter onset Uc = √[(ζs + ζlink) / a]
growth rate σ = −ω ζnet,  θ ∝ eσt while small
limit cycle (saturation β): Θ = √(−4 ζnet / β)

Vortex resonance: the air adds no damping, and M = Q U² sin(2π fs t) with the shedding frequency fs = St·U/D.

Θ = (Q U² / ω²) / √[(1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²],  r = fs / f

Time histories are integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta, 100 steps per period.

Calibration. These are teaching models tuned to the 1940 observations, not a reconstruction of the bridge's aerodynamics.

f = 0.2 Hz (12 cycles/min), D = 8 ft girder depth, B = 39 ft
a set so Uc = 35 mph with ζs = 0.5 %, link slipped
β set so Θ = 0.7 rad (40°) at 42 mph
St = 0.13, giving fs ≈ 1 Hz at 42 mph
Q set so vortex resonance gives Θ = 3° at its tuned wind
ζlink = 1.5 % while the midspan link holds (assumed)

The structural damping, the link's effect and the saturation law are assumptions chosen for illustration. Change them to see which conclusions depend on them: the frequency mismatch and the growth of flutter with wind speed do not.
